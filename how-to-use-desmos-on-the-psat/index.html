How to Use Desmos on the PSAT: Three Rules That Work for a Large Portion of the PSAT Math Module

By Dr. Brink, Ph.D., 32 years teaching mathematics

The most powerful thing you can do with Desmos on the PSAT is turn any equation into a graph. Graph the left side as its own equation, graph the right side as its own equation, and the point where the two graphs cross is the solution. It works on almost every equation the test gives you, and it is faster and more accurate than grinding through the algebra by hand.

1. Graph both sides. Where they cross is the answer.

Any time you see an equation, do not solve it. Split it in two. Type the left side as one equation and the right side as another, and read off the point where they meet.

EXAMPLE 1

Solve 3x + 7 = 25.

In Desmos, type y = 3x + 7 and y = 25. The two lines cross at x = 6. That is your answer, and it took about ten seconds.

The real value shows up when equations contain fractions and the algebra then gets messy. Desmos handles fractions just as easily as integers, so the answers come to you clean, quickly and correct.

EXAMPLE 2

Solve 2x/3 − 1/4 = x/2 + 1/6.

By hand you would hunt for a common denominator, clear the fractions and hope nothing slips. In Desmos, type y = 2x/3 - 1/4 and y = x/2 + 1/6. The two lines cross at x = 2.5. No common denominators, no arithmetic slips.

It works just as well on curves.

EXAMPLE 3

Solve x² − 4x = 12.

By hand you would move everything over and factor. In Desmos, type y = x^2 - 4x and y = 12. The parabola crosses the line at x = −2 and x = 6. Both solutions, no factoring, no sign errors.

This is the whole idea. You are not solving the equation, you are letting the two graphs find the answer for you.

2. If there is an extra letter in any equation, use a slider.

Some PSAT questions put a second letter in the equation, an a or a k, and ask you to find its value. When you type an equation with a letter Desmos does not recognize, it offers to add a slider. Add it, then drag it until the graph does what the question is asking for.

EXAMPLE

The line y = kx + 2 passes through the point (4, 14). Find k.

Type y = kx + 2 and add the slider for k. Also type the point (4, 14). Now drag the slider until the line runs straight through the point. It lands on k = 3.

3. Reading how many solutions there are, straight off the picture.

A whole family of PSAT questions asks how many real solutions an equation has, or how many times a graph meets a line. Once both sides are graphed, the picture tells you at a glance. The rule:

Graphs that cross have two real solutions. Graphs that just touch have one. Graphs that never meet have none.

Another thing to keep in mind: solutions, roots and intersections are close synonyms of each other. The PSAT question might use any of these, but they essentially mean the same thing, and the graphical solutions tell the same story.

EXAMPLE

How many real solutions does x² + 2x + 5 = 0 have?

Graph y = x^2 + 2x + 5 and y = 0 (the x-axis). The parabola sits entirely above the axis and never touches it. No intersection, no real solutions, no roots. You did not need to calculate a discriminant, because the graph told the story very quickly.

Compare three quick cases. x² − 4 = 0 crosses the axis twice, at −2 and 2, so two solutions (roots). x² = 0 touches the axis once, at the origin, so one solution (root). x² + 2x + 5 = 0 never reaches it, so no solutions (roots). Crosses, touches, misses.

One last thing: know when to put Desmos down.

Desmos is built for equations and graphs, and that is where it wins. For a quick piece of arithmetic or a geometry question that is really about an angle or a rule, your pencil is often faster than typing it in. Use the calculator where it saves you time, not on everything. The students who gain the most are the ones who know which questions to hand to it.

On the PSAT, these habits pay twice: they raise your score now, and they carry straight over to the SAT.
