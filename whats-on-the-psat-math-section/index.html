What's on the PSAT Math Section? Format, Timing and Topics

By Dr. Brink, Ph.D., 32 years teaching mathematics

The PSAT math section has 44 questions in two modules of 22. Each module takes 35 minutes, so 70 minutes in all. It is taken on a computer with the Desmos graphing calculator built in, and it is scored from 160 to 760. About a quarter of the questions ask you to type your own answer instead of choosing from four options.

How the section is built

The math section comes in two parts, called modules. Everyone gets a similar first module. How you do on that first module decides whether your second module is easier or harder. That is called adaptive testing, and it means the first module matters more than most students realize. A careless slip early on can steer you into the easier second module, and the easier module has a lower ceiling on your score.

What you get on screen

You have the Desmos graphing calculator for the whole section, a reference sheet of formulas, a tool for crossing out answer choices, and scratch paper. You do not need to bring your own calculator, although you are allowed to.

The four content areas

Every question belongs to one of four areas. The approximate number of questions in each:

Content areaQuestionsWhat it covers
Algebra13 to 15Linear equations, inequalities, systems of equations and linear functions
Advanced Math12 to 14Quadratics, exponential functions, polynomials and equivalent expressions
Problem-Solving and Data Analysis7 to 9Ratios, rates, percentages, units, and reading data from tables and graphs
Geometry and Trigonometry4 to 6Area, volume, angles, triangles, circles and right-triangle trigonometry

Algebra and Advanced Math together make up well over half of the section. That is where most of the points are, and it is where most students should spend most of their time.

What the questions actually test

Behind every PSAT question sits a set of earlier skills, many of them from 7th, 8th and 9th grade. A student who loses points on a quadratic question is often not missing anything about quadratics. They are missing something underneath it, such as distributing a negative sign or working with fractions. That is why simply doing more practice questions often does not move a score. Finding the specific skill underneath does.

How to use this

Learn the format before test day so nothing on the screen surprises you. Practice with Desmos so it is a habit, not an experiment. And when you miss a question, ask whether you did not know how to do it, or knew how and made a slip, because the fix is different for each.

The years before the test matter most

Last but not least, how a student moves through the early years of math matters enormously. The PSAT math section is the result of years of skills stacked on top of each other like Lego blocks. If a piece is missing, the whole structure can come tumbling down.
