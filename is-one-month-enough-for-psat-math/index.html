Is One Month Enough to Prepare for PSAT Math?

By Dr. Brink, Ph.D., 32 years teaching mathematics

One month is enough to raise your PSAT math score, if you spend it on the right things. A month is not enough to relearn all of middle and high school math, and you do not need to. It is enough to find the specific mistakes costing you points and fix those, which is where most of the gain is. A second, smaller gain comes from getting familiar with the PSAT testing platform: knowing what tools are on screen and how to use them.

Why a month works

Most students do not lose points evenly across the test. They lose them to a small number of repeating mistakes: a negative sign dropped when distributing, a question read too fast, a fraction step that never quite set. Each of those mistakes shows up on several questions. Fix one, and you win back points on all of them at once. That is why a focused month beats a vague semester.

Why a month often fails

The month fails when it is spent doing random practice questions. A student does fifty questions, gets thirty right, feels busy, and changes nothing. The thirty they got right did not need practice, and the twenty they missed were never looked at closely. Doing more questions is not the same as fixing what goes wrong.

What a good month looks like

  1. Week 1. Rebuild the basics and take one full practice test under real conditions. The test is not for the score. It is for finding out exactly what goes wrong.
  2. Week 2. Work only on what the test showed you. Go through every missed question, name the mistake, and practice that skill until it is automatic. Learn to use Desmos properly, because it turns many equations into a ten-second graph.
  3. Week 3. Take a second full test. Some mistakes will be gone. Work on the ones still there, and on the earlier skills sitting underneath them.
  4. Week 4. Full tests, close to test day, so the timing and the screen feel normal.

The rule that matters most

Do a little every day. Thirty focused minutes a day for a month is worth far more than a few long Saturdays that never quite happen. The students who improve most are not the ones who study hardest on one weekend. They are the ones who show up every day. I have written about this as the 20-mile march.

And work every problem on paper, showing your steps. Mental shortcuts are where most slips happen, and you cannot fix a slip you cannot see.
