What Is a Good PSAT Math Score? Benchmarks and Rankings by Grade

By Dr. Brink, Ph.D., 32 years teaching mathematics

The short answer: a good PSAT math score is one at or above the College Board benchmark for your grade, which is 480 in 10th grade and 510 in 11th grade. The math section is scored from 160 to 760. For juniors, a math score around 480 is roughly the middle of everyone who takes the test, about 600 is roughly the top 10 to 15 percent, and 700 or higher is roughly the top 5 percent.

Parents and students ask me this every October, usually right after the score report arrives and nobody is quite sure whether the number on it is good news or bad news. This page explains how the math score works, what the official benchmarks mean, roughly where each score ranks, and how much the math score matters for National Merit.

How the PSAT math score works

The PSAT/NMSQT math section has 44 questions in two modules of 22, with 35 minutes for each module. The first module is the same for everyone. The second module adapts: it gets harder if the first module went well and easier if it did not, and the harder second module can reach higher scores. The Desmos graphing calculator and a formula reference sheet are on screen for every question.

The math score runs from 160 to 760, in steps of 10. It is added to the Reading and Writing score, which uses the same scale, for a total from 320 to 1520. The PSAT tops out at 760 rather than 800 because it is written for 10th and 11th graders, so a 760 is a perfect PSAT math score.

The official benchmarks

The College Board publishes a benchmark for each grade. A student at or above the benchmark is considered on track for college and career readiness, meaning they are likely to do well in a first-year college math course if they keep growing at a normal pace.

Roughly where each score ranks for juniors

A benchmark tells you whether you are on track. A percentile tells you how you compare with everyone else. These are approximate, because the College Board updates its percentile tables every year, but they give an honest picture for 11th graders.

For sophomores, the same score ranks higher, because 10th graders have had a year less of math. A 10th grader with a 550 is doing better, relative to classmates, than a junior with a 550.

What the math score means for National Merit

Only the PSAT/NMSQT taken in 11th grade counts for the National Merit Scholarship Program. The program uses a Selection Index built from your two section scores:

Selection Index = (2 × Reading and Writing + Math) ÷ 10

The index runs from 48 to 228. Notice that Reading and Writing is counted twice and Math only once. So math makes up one third of the Selection Index, not half. A student hoping for National Merit recognition needs a high math score, but a 10-point gain in Reading and Writing moves the index twice as far as a 10-point gain in math. The cutoffs are set separately for each state and change every year.

If your score is lower than you hoped

In 32 years of teaching I have found that a low PSAT math score almost never means a student is "bad at math." It usually means a small number of specific mistakes are repeating across many questions. A student who misreads what a question is asking, or who drops a negative sign when distributing, loses the same points again and again. Other times the problem sits underneath the question, in a skill from 7th or 8th grade that never quite set, such as working with fractions or solving a two-step equation.

That is good news, because a repeating mistake can be found and fixed. The most useful thing to do with a PSAT score is not to stare at the number, but to find out which mistakes produced it.

  • Look at the questions you missed and sort them into content areas: Algebra, Advanced Math, Problem-Solving and Data Analysis, and Geometry and Trigonometry.
  • For each missed question, ask whether you did not know how to do it, or knew how and made a slip. The fix is different for each.
  • Work each practice problem out properly on paper, showing as many steps as you can. Mental shortcuts are where most slips happen.
  • Learn to use the on-screen Desmos calculator. Many PSAT questions can be checked, or solved outright, by graphing.
